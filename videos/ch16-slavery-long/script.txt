# 0. Cold open
In April of eighteen sixty-one, the United States went to war with itself.
Over six hundred thousand Americans would die.
Here is the strange part.
Roughly three out of four white southerners did not own a single enslaved person.
So why would a region fight, and bleed, for a system most of its people had no direct stake in?
To answer that, you have to go back seventy years, to a machine, a crop, and a prediction that turned out to be wrong.
This is Chapter Sixteen: slavery in the United States, seventeen ninety-three to eighteen sixty.

# 1. The prediction that failed
In the seventeen eighties, slavery looked like it might die on its own.
Revolutionary idealism was in the air, and some southern leaders, Jefferson among them, talked openly about freeing their slaves.
Others made a colder argument: economics would eventually expose slavery as unprofitable, and it would fade.
The North was already moving.
Vermont banned slavery in its constitution in seventeen seventy-seven, Massachusetts in seventeen eighty, and Pennsylvania, New York, and New Jersey passed gradual emancipation laws by eighteen oh four.
The Northwest Ordinance of seventeen eighty-seven kept slavery out of the Old Northwest entirely.
Then, in seventeen ninety-three, Eli Whitney built a cotton gin.
Before the gin, separating seeds from short-staple cotton was so slow that the crop barely paid.
After it, one worker could clean fifty times as much in a day.
Suddenly cotton was worth planting everywhere it would grow, and every new field needed labor to work it.
The numbers tell the story.
In seventeen ninety, the South produced about a thousand tons of cotton a year.
By eighteen sixty, it was a million tons.
In the same seventy years, the enslaved population grew from five hundred thousand to four million.
The prediction had been that slavery would wither.
Instead, the gin gave it a second life.

# 2. Cotton is King
By the eighteen forties, southerners had a phrase for this: cotton is king.
Look at what cotton meant to the national economy.
In eighteen hundred, cotton was seven percent of American exports.
By eighteen twenty, thirty-two percent.
By eighteen forty, it crossed half, and by eighteen sixty it was fifty-seven and a half percent of everything the United States sold abroad.
The South grew more than half of the entire world's supply.
That gave southern leaders a sense of leverage.
Britain's textile mills ran on southern cotton, so southerners believed that if war ever came, Britain would have to take their side.
But the money did not stay in the South.
Northern shippers carried the cotton and took large profits.
Northern bankers lent the planters money.
New England mills spun the fiber.
The prosperity of the North, the South, and England, as the textbook puts it, rested on the backs of enslaved people.
Hold on to that, because it explains something later: why so many northerners, who disliked slavery, were in no hurry to end it.

# 3. The Cotton Kingdom moves west
Put two maps side by side.
In eighteen twenty, cotton production hugs the Atlantic coast: South Carolina, Georgia, a few river valleys.
By eighteen sixty, a wide band of red runs from the Carolinas through Georgia and into Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana.
Historians call this the black belt, named for the dark, fertile soil, and for the enslaved population that was forced to work it.
In some of these counties, enslaved people were more than half the population.
In Mississippi and South Carolina they were the majority of the entire state.
Where did these four million people come from?
Not from Africa, mostly.
Congress outlawed the legal importation of enslaved Africans in eighteen oh eight, a year after Britain abolished its own slave trade.
Smuggling continued, but it was a small share.
Most of the growth came from natural increase: children born into slavery.
And because the Atlantic trade was closed, a vigorous internal slave trade grew up in its place.
Enslaved people were sold from the older states of the upper South down to the new cotton lands of the Deep South.
By eighteen sixty, planters held roughly two billion dollars of capital in human beings.
That was the single largest form of wealth in the region, larger than the land itself.

# 4. Who owned whom
Now the question from the beginning: who actually owned slaves?
Fewer people than you might think.
In eighteen fifty, only one thousand seven hundred thirty-three families in the entire South owned a hundred or more enslaved people.
This tiny group was the planter aristocracy.
They held the bulk of southern wealth and provided nearly all of its political and social leadership.
Below them were tens of thousands of smaller slaveholders, most owning fewer than ten people.
Add them all together and about one in four white southerners either owned slaves or belonged to a family that did.
Which means three in four did not.
Picture southern society in eighteen fifty as a pyramid.
At the very top, the slavocracy: the plantation owners.
Below them, the plain folk: around six million white yeoman farmers, most with no slaves at all.
Below them, about two hundred fifty thousand free Black people.
At the base, three million two hundred thousand enslaved people.
In total, the South held about nine and a quarter million people, forty percent of the national population.
So back to the question.
The plain folk had no direct economic stake in slavery.
Yet they were among its stoutest defenders.
Why?
Partly hope: a poor farmer could imagine buying one enslaved person and rising, the southern version of the American dream.
Partly fear: as long as four million people were enslaved, even the poorest white man was not at the bottom.
Slavery gave him a rung to stand on.
Take that away, and some poorer whites would have been, as the textbook says, no better off than the slaves.

# 5. A fragile kingdom
The Cotton Kingdom looked mighty, but it had cracks.
The economy was built on one crop.
When world cotton prices fell, the whole region fell with them.
Planters overspeculated in land and in people, borrowing against future harvests.
An enslaved workforce was a heavy capital investment, and a single disease outbreak could wipe out an entire slave quarter.
And the system kept something else out: immigrants.
Europeans heading to America went North, where there were wages and cheap land.
In eighteen sixty, only four point four percent of the southern population was foreign-born, against eighteen point seven percent in the North.
Germans and Irish who did look South found themselves competing with slave labor, priced out of fertile land, and ignorant of cotton farming.
So the North gained manpower and factories.
The South gained more cotton fields.
Southern planters watched the North grow rich off their crop and resented every dollar of it.

# 6. Free Blacks
Not every Black southerner was enslaved.
By eighteen sixty about two hundred fifty thousand free Black people lived in the South.
Some traced their freedom to the idealism of the Revolution, when some owners had emancipated their slaves.
Some had purchased their own freedom.
Many were of mixed ancestry.
Some owned property, even, in a few cases, slaves.
But they lived as what the textbook calls a third race, hemmed in by restrictions.
And the North was not a refuge.
Several northern states forbade free Black people from entering at all.
Most denied them the vote.
Some barred them from public schools.
Irish immigrants, competing for the same low-wage jobs, were especially hostile.
One observer put the contrast this way: southerners liked Black people as individuals but despised the race, while white northerners professed to like the race but disliked individual Black people.

# 7. Life under the lash
So how did enslaved people actually live?
Conditions varied enormously, from the sugar parishes of Louisiana to a household in Virginia.
But some things were constant.
Enslaved people had no political rights and almost no legal protection.
They could not testify in court.
Their marriages were not legally recognized, which meant a family could be split at a sale with no legal recourse.
Floggings were common.
Enslaved people who resisted were sometimes sent to professional breakers.
Even the economic logic was brutal: lash marks hurt resale value, so some owners restrained the whip not out of mercy but to protect the investment.
The auction was the heart of the horror.
Families were separated: a mother sold to Mississippi, her child kept in Virginia.
Abolitionists called this slavery's greatest psychological cruelty, and Harriet Beecher Stowe built Uncle Tom's Cabin around it in eighteen fifty-two.
And yet.
Most enslaved people lived on larger plantations of twenty or more, where family life was relatively stable.
Most were raised in two-parent households.
A distinctive African American culture took shape: music, folk tales, and a Christianity reshaped during the Second Great Awakening into call and response preaching.
Survival sometimes required a mask.
Enslaved people learned to perform what white observers called the Sambo pattern, the grinning, harmless caricature that whites wanted to see.
A former slave named John Little explained it better than any historian.
They say slaves are happy, he wrote, because they laugh and are merry.
He and three others had received two hundred lashes in a day, with their feet in fetters.
Yet at night, he said, we would sing and dance, and make others laugh at the rattling of our chains.
We did it to keep down trouble, and to keep our hearts from being completely broken.
Happy men we must have been, he wrote.
Yet I have done it myself. I have cut capers in chains.
When a textbook in nineteen thirty-two could still call slavery the Negro's necessary transition to civilization, remember John Little.
The conditions of slavery matter.
The existence of slavery matters more.

# 8. Resistance and rebellion
Enslaved people resisted every day, in ways that rarely made the history books.
They ran away.
They slowed the pace of work.
They took food from the big house, broke tools, and occasionally poisoned a master's meal.
And sometimes they rose up.
In eighteen hundred, an enslaved man named Gabriel organized an armed insurrection in Richmond, Virginia.
Informers betrayed it, and the leaders were hanged.
In eighteen twenty-two, Denmark Vesey, a free Black man in Charleston, planned a rebellion.
Informers again.
Vesey and about thirty followers were publicly hanged.
Then, in August of eighteen thirty-one, Nat Turner.
A semiliterate preacher who claimed religious visions, Turner gathered around seventy followers in Southampton County, Virginia, and went from plantation to plantation.
Around sixty white Virginians were killed.
The uprising was crushed within days, and Turner and perhaps eighteen others were hanged.
Eight years later, enslaved Africans seized the Spanish ship Amistad at sea.
None of these rebellions could succeed.
They lacked numbers, weapons, and organization.
As one historian puts it, the only way a rebellion could have worked was if the government had aided the slaves, which would have meant the government, not the slaves, was in control.
Every right enslaved people ever received came from those in power, and those in power made decisions based on profit, not on whether it was right.
Turner's revolt failed as a rebellion.
As a shock to the South, it succeeded completely.

# 9. Abolition
Opposition to slavery was as old as the Revolution.
Quakers had condemned it in the seventeen seventies.
The first organized plan was not freedom in America but removal from it.
In eighteen seventeen the American Colonization Society proposed sending Black Americans back to Africa.
In eighteen twenty-two it founded the Republic of Liberia on the West African coast.
Over four decades about fifteen thousand people went.
Most had no wish to go.
By eighteen sixty the great majority of enslaved people were native-born Americans with their own history and culture, and Africa was a foreign continent.
The eighteen thirties brought a harder edge.
William Lloyd Garrison launched The Liberator, a newspaper that refused to tolerate what he called the poisonous weed of slavery and vowed to stamp it out, root and branch.
In eighteen thirty-three he and Wendell Phillips founded the American Anti-Slavery Society.
Black abolitionists were already ahead of him.
David Walker's Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World, in eighteen twenty-nine, called for a bloody end to white supremacy.
Sojourner Truth fought for emancipation and for women's rights at the same time.
Martin Delany was one of the few Black leaders to take recolonization seriously.
And then there was Frederick Douglass.
Douglass escaped bondage in eighteen thirty-eight at twenty-one.
In eighteen forty-one he gave an impromptu speech at an antislavery meeting in Massachusetts, and the abolitionists discovered him.
He lectured despite beatings, and in eighteen forty-five published his Narrative, the classic American autobiography of slavery.
Garrison burned a copy of the Constitution on the Fourth of July, eighteen fifty-four, calling it a covenant with death and an agreement with hell.
Douglass took a different road: he looked to politics.
He backed the Liberty Party in eighteen forty, the Free Soil Party in eighteen forty-eight, and the Republican Party in the eighteen fifties.
Knowledge, Douglass said, makes a man unfit to be a slave.

# 10. The South lashes back
Before eighteen thirty, some southerners still debated slavery, at least as a necessary evil.
After eighteen thirty, the debate ended.
What changed?
Nat Turner, first of all.
The revolt sent waves of hysteria through the South.
Planters slept with pistols.
Georgia offered five thousand dollars for Garrison's arrest, blaming him for inciting murder.
Then the Nullification Crisis of eighteen thirty-two, when President Jackson denied South Carolina's right to nullify a federal law, deepened southern fears about federal power.
Northern criticism stung.
And southern preachers supplied an answer: the Bible never clearly condemned slavery, and religious leaders in scripture had owned slaves, so the institution must be acceptable.
The result was a new argument.
Slavery was not a necessary evil.
Slavery was a positive good.
The master-slave relationship, apologists claimed, resembled a family.
Enslaved people, they said, were happier than the overworked northern wage slaves.
Slave states tightened their slave codes and moved to prohibit emancipation of any kind, even voluntary.
Jailings, whippings, and lynchings ended what remained of the southern debate.
In Congress, southerners pushed through a gag resolution that tabled every antislavery petition without debate.
In eighteen thirty-five southern postmasters were ordered to destroy abolitionist mail.
Free speech itself had become a sectional issue.
And the North?
Most northerners disliked the abolitionists, especially the Garrisonians.
They revered the Constitution's compromises on slavery.
Southern planters owed northern bankers about three hundred million dollars, money that would vanish if the Union broke.
But through the eighteen fifties the abolitionist outcry made a dent in the northern mind.
More and more northerners came to see the South as the land of the unfree.

# 11. Conclusion
Every proslavery argument widened the chasm.
Every abolitionist pamphlet widened it from the other side.
Into that gap stepped Abraham Lincoln, who, in the historian Howard Zinn's words, combined perfectly the needs of business, the ambition of the new Republican Party, and the rhetoric of humanitarianism.
He would keep abolition not at the top of his list, but close enough that pressure could push it there.
Which brings us back to the question the chapter opened with.
Can a nation dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal long endure, while four million of its people are property?
In eighteen sixty-one, the country would find out.
